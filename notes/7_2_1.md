## 1. Why is $T \neq 0$ treatment different from $T = 0$?

**Q:** Why is the treatment of condensed matter theory problems and their ground state calculations at $T \neq 0$ different compared to those with $T = 0$?

**A:** The root cause is the nature of the state being described.

At $T = 0$ the system occupies a **single pure state** $|\psi_0\rangle$. At $T \neq 0$ it is in a **statistical mixture** over all energy eigenstates, weighted by Boltzmann factors:

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z(\beta)^{-1} e^{-\beta\hat{H}}, \qquad \langle \hat{A}\rangle_\beta = \frac{1}{Z}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{A}\right]$$

| Aspect | $T = 0$ | $T \neq 0$ |
|---|---|---|
| **State** | Pure state $\|\psi_0\rangle$ | Mixed state $\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ |
| **Observables** | $\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \langle\psi_0\|\hat{A}\|\psi_0\rangle$ | $\langle\hat{A}\rangle_\beta = Z^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{A}]$ — sum over **all** eigenstates |
| **Hilbert space** | Physical chain $P$ only | Doubled system $P \otimes Q$ via **purification** |
| **MPS bond dim** | $D$ | $D^2$ (purification doubles it) |
| **Computational cost** | $O(D^3)$ | $O(D^6)$ |
| **Algorithm** | Variational DMRG ground state search | Imaginary time evolution $e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\|\psi_0\rangle$ |

**Why purification?** A mixed state on physical space $P$ is interpreted as the partial trace of a pure state on $P \otimes Q$, where $Q$ is an auxiliary copy of the chain. The thermal density operator $\hat{\rho}_\beta$ is then obtained by imaginary time evolution $|\psi_\beta\rangle = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle$.

**The $T \to \infty$ problem:** At high temperatures, many states contribute at similar but not identical weight, and MPS are not efficient at encoding this wealth of contributing states.

> **Summary:** $T=0$ is a single-vector problem in $\mathcal{H}$; $T\neq 0$ is a matrix problem in $\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}$, requiring twice the space, twice the entanglement budget, and an imaginary-time "cool-down" to reach the correct thermal state. — *Schollwöck §7.2.1*

## 2. How do we know $T=0$ gives a single pure state?

**Q:** How do I know that at $T=0$ the system occupies a single pure state?

**A:** This follows directly from the mathematics of the thermal density matrix.

Expand $\hat{\rho}_\beta$ in the energy eigenbasis $\hat{H}|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$, with $E_0 \leq E_1 \leq E_2 \leq \ldots$:

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = \frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}}}{Z(\beta)} = \frac{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}|n\rangle\langle n|}{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}}$$

Factor out $e^{-\beta E_0}$:

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = \frac{|0\rangle\langle 0| + \sum_{n>0} e^{-\beta(E_n - E_0)}|n\rangle\langle n|}{1 + \sum_{n>0} e^{-\beta(E_n - E_0)}}$$

As $\beta = 1/k_BT \to \infty$, every excited-state term is exponentially suppressed by the **gap** $\Delta = E_1 - E_0 > 0$:

$$e^{-\beta(E_n - E_0)} \xrightarrow{\beta\to\infty} 0, \quad n > 0 \implies \hat{\rho}_{\beta\to\infty} \to |0\rangle\langle 0|$$

**Critical caveats:**

| Condition | What happens at $T\to 0$ |
|---|---|
| Non-degenerate ground state + gap $\Delta > 0$ | Pure state $\|0\rangle\langle 0\|$ ✓ |
| $g$-fold degenerate ground state | Mixed state $\frac{1}{g}\sum_{i=1}^g\|0_i\rangle\langle 0_i\|$ |
| Gapless / critical system ($\Delta\to 0$) | Pure state in principle, but convergence is **slow** |

*(Pathria & Beale §2.3; Huang §7.3)*

## 3. Why Boltzmann factors and not Gibbs factors?

**Q:** Why is the thermal state weighted by Boltzmann factors and not Gibbs factors?

**A:** It is an **ensemble choice**, not a fundamental law.

| Ensemble | Fixed | Fluctuating | Weight | Partition function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Canonical** | $N, V, T$ | Energy | $e^{-\beta E_n}$ (Boltzmann) | $Z_C = \text{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}]$ |
| **Grand canonical** | $\mu, V, T$ | Energy + particles | $e^{-\beta(E_n - \mu N_n)}$ (Gibbs) | $\mathcal{Z} = \text{Tr}[e^{-\beta(\hat{H}-\mu\hat{N})}]$ |

Schollwöck writes $e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ because he presents the **canonical** case (fixed particle number $N$) for simplicity — appropriate for a closed spin chain. In the **thermodynamic limit**, the two ensembles give identical results for bulk observables. *(Huang §7.6)*

## 5. What does $T \neq 0$ mean for a many-body system?

**Q:** What is the meaning of $T\neq 0$ temperature of a many-body system? It's not like we can take a thermometer and measure the temperature of a spin chain.

**A:** Temperature is **not intrinsic** to an isolated quantum system. An isolated spin chain in a pure state $|\psi\rangle$ has no temperature — it is a well-defined quantum state. Temperature only becomes meaningful when the system is coupled to an **environment** (a heat bath).

**Where $\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ comes from:**

Start from the total system = spin chain $S$ + large bath $B$, treated as microcanonical (fixed total energy $E_\text{tot}$). Tracing out the bath degrees of freedom, and Taylor-expanding $\ln\Omega_B$ around $E_\text{tot}$:

$$\hat{\rho}_S = \text{Tr}_B\,\hat{\rho}_\text{tot} = \frac{1}{Z}e^{-\beta \hat{H}_S}, \qquad \beta \equiv \left.\frac{\partial \ln \Omega_B}{\partial E}\right|_{E_\text{tot}}$$

**Temperature $T = 1/k_B\beta$ is a property of the bath**, not the spin chain itself.

**Mahan's formulation** (*Many-Particle Physics*):
> "At nonzero temperature, it is assumed there is something with a nonzero temperature. That is, our particle, whether electron, phonon, or spin, is interacting with a bath of other particles which have an average energy. The exact state of all these other particles is not known, since they are fluctuating between different configurations. All that is known is the temperature, which is related to the mean energy."

| Mahan's words | Formal meaning |
|---|---|
| *"interacting with a bath of other particles"* | System $S$ weakly coupled to bath $B$ |
| *"exact state not known"* | We trace out $B$: $\hat{\rho}_S = \text{Tr}_B\, \hat{\rho}_\text{tot}$ |
| *"fluctuating between different configurations"* | The bath explores its microstates — forces the sum $\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}\|n\rangle\langle n\|$ |
| *"all that is known is the temperature"* | The only surviving parameter after tracing out $B$ is $\beta = \partial \ln\Omega_B / \partial E$ |

**Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH)** *(Deutsch 1991; Srednicki 1994)*: For a generic (non-integrable) many-body system, a single energy eigenstate $|n\rangle$ at energy density $\epsilon$ already gives thermal expectation values for all local observables.

| System type | ETH holds? | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Generic / non-integrable | Yes | Individual eigenstates look thermal |
| Integrable (e.g. XXZ) | No | Relaxes to Generalized Gibbs Ensemble |
| Many-body localized (MBL) | No | Never thermalizes |

## 6. Derivation of the reduced density matrix from the microcanonical ensemble

**Q:** How do you get $\hat{\rho}_S = Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_S}$ by doing the trace of the total density operator?

**A:** The exponential comes from a Taylor expansion of the bath's density of states.

**Setup:** $\hat{H}_\text{tot} = \hat{H}_S + \hat{H}_B$, eigenstates $\hat{H}_S|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$, $\hat{H}_B|\alpha\rangle = \varepsilon_\alpha|\alpha\rangle$.

**Step 1 — Compute $\text{Tr}_B$:**

$$(\hat{\rho}_S)_{mn} = \sum_\alpha \langle m,\alpha|\hat{\rho}_\text{tot}|n,\alpha\rangle = \frac{\delta_{mn}}{\Omega(E_\text{tot})}\sum_\alpha \delta(E_m + \varepsilon_\alpha - E_\text{tot})$$

- $\delta_{mn}$: $\hat{\rho}_S$ is diagonal — no off-diagonal coherences survive
- The sum counts bath states with energy $E_\text{tot} - E_m$: this is $\Omega_B(E_\text{tot} - E_m)$

$$(\hat{\rho}_S)_{mm} = \frac{\Omega_B(E_\text{tot} - E_m)}{\Omega(E_\text{tot})}$$

**Step 2 — Taylor expand** (source of the exponential):

The bath is large: $E_m \ll E_\text{tot}$. Expand $\ln\Omega_B$ (smooth) rather than $\Omega_B$ (wildly varying):

$$\ln\Omega_B(E_\text{tot} - E_m) \approx \ln\Omega_B(E_\text{tot}) - E_m \underbrace{\frac{\partial \ln\Omega_B}{\partial E}\bigg|_{E_\text{tot}}}_{\equiv\, \beta}$$

Exponentiating: $\Omega_B(E_\text{tot} - E_m) \approx \Omega_B(E_\text{tot})\cdot e^{-\beta E_m}$

**Step 3 — Substitute back:**

$$(\hat{\rho}_S)_{mm} = C\cdot e^{-\beta E_m}, \qquad C = \frac{\Omega_B(E_\text{tot})}{\Omega(E_\text{tot})}$$

**Step 4 — Normalize:**

$$C\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m} = 1 \implies C = \frac{1}{Z}, \quad Z \equiv \sum_m e^{-\beta E_m}$$

**Result:** $\hat{\rho}_S = Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_S}$

The exponential is not put in by hand — it is the direct consequence of $\ln\Omega_B$ being linear in energy over the range of $E_m$ (i.e., the bath being large). *(Pathria & Beale §3.1–3.2; Huang §8.1)*

## 7. Mini-lecture outline: Schollwöck §7.2

### Block 1 — Motivation

- At $T=0$: pure state $|\psi_0\rangle$ → MPS machinery applies directly
- At $T\neq 0$: mixed state $\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ — not a wavefunction
- Central question: how do we represent and time-evolve a mixed state using MPS?

### Block 2 — Purification: turning a mixed state into a pure state (Eq. 234)

Any mixed state $\hat{\rho}^P$ on physical space $P$ can be written as the partial trace of a pure state on $P \otimes Q$:

$$\hat{\rho}^P = \sum_a s_a^2 |a\rangle_P\langle a|_P \quad\longrightarrow\quad |\psi\rangle = \sum_a s_a|a\rangle_P|a\rangle_Q, \quad \hat{\rho}^P = \text{Tr}_Q|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$$

$Q$ is chosen as an identical copy of $P$ — the chain becomes a **ladder**.

### Block 3 — Reaching $\hat{\rho}_\beta$ via imaginary time evolution (Eq. 235–237)

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z(\beta)^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}} = Z(\beta)^{-1}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\cdot\underbrace{\hat{I}}_{=\,Z(0)\hat{\rho}_0}\cdot e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}$$

Pulling the trace over $Q$ out gives:

$$|\psi_\beta\rangle = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle$$

This is **imaginary time evolution** with $\tau = \beta/2$.

### Block 4 — The infinite-temperature starting state (Eq. 241–244)

$\hat{\rho}_0 = d^{-L}\hat{I}$ factorizes → its purification is a **product state** (MPS of bond dimension $D=1$). Each rung is a maximally entangled state:

$$|\psi^i_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_\sigma |\sigma\rangle_P|\sigma\rangle_Q$$

For spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ chains with good quantum numbers: use the singlet:
$$|\psi^i_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[|\!\uparrow_P\downarrow_Q\rangle - |\!\downarrow_P\uparrow_Q\rangle\right]$$

### Block 5 — Expectation values and thermodynamics (Eq. 238–240)

$$\langle\hat{O}\rangle_\beta = \frac{\langle\psi_\beta|\hat{O}|\psi_\beta\rangle}{\langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle}$$

- Reduces to a **pure state expectation value**
- Norm $\langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle = Z(\beta)/Z(0)$ gives the partition function
- From $Z(\beta)$: free energy $F = -\beta^{-1}\ln Z(\beta)$, internal energy $U = \langle\hat{H}\rangle_\beta$, entropy $S = \beta(U-F)$

### Block 6 — MPO for the ladder (§7.2.2)

The ladder maps to a chain. $\hat{H}$ acts only on odd (physical) sites → next-nearest-neighbour interactions on the mapped chain.

| Strategy | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Direct MPO** | 4-site Trotter blocks $(1,2,3,4), (5,6,7,8),\ldots$ |
| **Swap gates** | Swap sites $2\leftrightarrow 3$, reducing to nearest-neighbour; swap back |

### Block 7 — Computational cost

| Quantity | $T=0$ | $T\neq 0$ (purification) |
|---|---|---|
| State space | Physical chain $P$ | Ladder $P\otimes Q$ |
| Bond dimension | $D$ | $D^2$ |
| Cost per step | $O(D^3)$ | $O(D^6)$ |

## 8. Why imaginary time and not real time evolution?

**Q:** Why do we need imaginary time evolution instead of real time evolution?

**A:** The thermal density operator $e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ is **not unitary** — it is a real exponential decay. Compare what the two evolutions do to a state $|\psi_0\rangle = \sum_n c_n|n\rangle$:

| Evolution | Action | Effect on weights |
|---|---|---|
| Real time: $e^{-i\hat{H}t}$ | $\sum_n c_n e^{-iE_n t}\|n\rangle$ | Phases rotate, $\|c_n\|$ **unchanged** |
| Imaginary time: $e^{-\tau\hat{H}}$ | $\sum_n c_n e^{-\tau E_n}\|n\rangle$ | High-$E_n$ states **exponentially suppressed** |

The Boltzmann weights $e^{-\beta E_n}$ are precisely what imaginary time with $\tau = \beta/2$ produces. Real time evolution is unitary — it can never change the relative weights of energy eigenstates, so it can never build a thermal distribution.

## 9. Why do we need purification? Can't we work directly with $\hat{\rho}_\beta$?

**Q:** Why do we need to rethink the problem with the help of purification as the first step?

**A:** You can work directly with $\hat{\rho}_\beta$ (Zwolak & Vidal 2004 did this), but it is less convenient.

Working directly with $\hat{\rho}_\beta$ as an MPO requires two physical indices per site $(\sigma_i, \sigma'_i)$ and imaginary time evolution must be applied from both sides simultaneously:

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\,\hat{\rho}_0\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}$$

This is mathematically equivalent to purification (Schollwöck [ref 51]), but purification is preferred because:

- It maps the mixed state problem **exactly back** to a pure state problem on a larger space
- The entire pure-state MPS toolkit (canonical forms, TEBD/tDMRG sweeps, expectation value contractions) is reused **without any modification**
- Expectation values become $\langle\hat{O}\rangle_\beta = \langle\psi_\beta|\hat{O}_P|\psi_\beta\rangle / \langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle$ — identical in form to a pure state

## 10. Why is taking $Q$ as a copy of $P$ not a loss of generality?

**Q:** Would taking the auxiliary space as a copy of $P$ lead to a loss of generality?

**A:** No — it is the **minimal choice that guarantees full generality**.

Given a mixed state $\hat{\rho}^P$ of Schmidt rank $r$, its purification requires $\dim(\mathcal{H}_Q) \geq r$. For a generic, full-rank density matrix on a chain of $L$ sites with local dimension $d$:

$$r_\text{max} = d^L = \dim(\mathcal{H}_P)$$

So the minimum auxiliary space that can always handle any density matrix is $\dim(\mathcal{H}_Q) = \dim(\mathcal{H}_P)$ — a copy of $P$.

**Non-uniqueness:** For any valid purification $|\psi\rangle$, so is $(\hat{I}_P \otimes \hat{U}_Q)|\psi\rangle$ for any unitary $\hat{U}_Q$ acting only on $Q$. This freedom allows switching from the maximally entangled state to the singlet — a computational advantage with no physical content.

## 11. Why is $e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ not unitary?

**Q:** Why is $e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ not unitary and what does the exponential suppression mean?

**A:**

An operator $\hat{U}$ is unitary iff $\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U} = \hat{I}$.

**Real time evolution:**
$$(e^{-i\hat{H}t})^\dagger = e^{+i\hat{H}t} \implies (e^{-i\hat{H}t})^\dagger(e^{-i\hat{H}t}) = \hat{I} \checkmark$$

**Imaginary time evolution** ($\hat{H}$ Hermitian, $\beta$ real):
$$(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})^\dagger = e^{-\beta\hat{H}} \implies (e^{-\beta\hat{H}})^\dagger(e^{-\beta\hat{H}}) = e^{-2\beta\hat{H}} \neq \hat{I} \times$$

In the energy eigenbasis: $e^{-\beta\hat{H}}|n\rangle = e^{-\beta E_n}|n\rangle$. Each eigenstate is scaled by $e^{-\beta E_n}$. After renormalization:

$$\frac{p_n}{p_m} = \frac{e^{-\beta E_n}}{e^{-\beta E_m}} = e^{-\beta(E_n - E_m)}$$

**Is suppression needed at $T=0$?** Yes — but for a different purpose. Imaginary time evolution is also the basis of ground state search:

$$e^{-\tau\hat{H}}|\psi_0\rangle = \sum_n c_n e^{-\tau E_n}|n\rangle \xrightarrow{\tau\to\infty} c_0 e^{-\tau E_0}|0\rangle$$

All excited states decay relative to the ground state by $e^{-\tau(E_n - E_0)} \to 0$.

## 12. Is imaginary time $\tau = it$ or $\tau = -it$?

**Q:** Is imaginary time $\tau = it$ or $\tau = -it$?

**A:** Start from real time evolution $e^{-i\hat{H}t}$ and substitute $t \to -i\tau$ with $\tau \in \mathbb{R}^+$:

$$e^{-i\hat{H}(-i\tau)} = e^{-\hat{H}\tau}$$

So the substitution is $t = -i\tau$, which means $\boxed{\tau = it}$.

This is the **Wick rotation**: rotating the time axis by $-90°$ in the complex plane. To reach the thermal state $e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}$ you need $\tau = \beta/2$, corresponding to $t = -i\beta/2$ — a purely imaginary real-time argument. This is exactly what Schollwöck means by "imaginary time evolution up to time $-i\beta/2$."

## 13. Why can't a unitary operator build a thermal distribution?

**Q:** Why can't real time evolution create a thermal distribution?

**A:** A unitary transformation $\hat{U}$ acting on a density matrix gives $\hat{\rho} \to \hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^\dagger$. This is a **similarity transformation**, which preserves all eigenvalues of $\hat{\rho}$.

**Case 1 — Starting from $\hat{\rho}_0 = d^{-L}\hat{I}$:**

$$\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_0\hat{U}^\dagger = d^{-L}\hat{U}\hat{U}^\dagger = d^{-L}\hat{I} = \hat{\rho}_0$$

A unitary on the maximally mixed state returns the maximally mixed state.

**Case 2 — Starting from a pure state $|\psi\rangle$:**

$$\hat{U}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\hat{U}^\dagger = |\phi\rangle\langle\phi|$$

Still a pure state. A thermal state at $T\neq 0$ is mixed (rank $> 1$). Unitaries cannot change the rank of a density matrix.

The **eigenvalue spectrum** of a density matrix encodes how mixed the state is — it is invariant under unitary transformations. Only non-unitary operations can change it: imaginary time evolution, partial traces, or measurements.

## 14. What is a thermal density operator?

**Q:** What is the meaning of a "thermal density operator"?

**A:** A **density operator** is the most general description of a quantum state, unifying quantum and classical uncertainty:

| Source of uncertainty | Description | Density operator |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantum** | System in a definite entangled state | Pure: $\hat{\rho} = \|\psi\rangle\langle\psi\|$ |
| **Classical** (statistical) | We don't know which state the system is in | Mixed: $\hat{\rho} = \sum_i p_i\|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i\|$ |

The **thermal density operator** is:

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = \frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad Z(\beta) = \text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}} = \sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}$$

Its three key limits:

| Limit | $\hat{\rho}_\beta$ | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $\beta \to \infty$ ($T\to 0$) | $\|0\rangle\langle 0\|$ | Pure ground state |
| Finite $\beta$ | $Z^{-1}\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}\|n\rangle\langle n\|$ | Mixture weighted by Boltzmann factors |
| $\beta \to 0$ ($T\to\infty$) | $d^{-L}\hat{I}$ | Maximally mixed — all eigenstates equally probable |

$\hat{\rho}_\beta$ is an **operator** on $\mathcal{H}$, not a vector in $\mathcal{H}$. Expectation values are computed as $\langle\hat{A}\rangle_\beta = \text{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_\beta\hat{A}]$ — not as $\langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle$. This is the fundamental reason the pure-state MPS machinery cannot be directly applied.

## 15. Why do we need the partition function normalization?

**Q:** What happens if I forget to normalize by the partition function?

**A:** A valid density operator must satisfy $\text{Tr}[\hat{\rho}] = 1$. Without $Z(\beta)$:

$$\text{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}] = \sum_n e^{-\beta E_n} = Z(\beta) \neq 1 \text{ in general}$$

The "probabilities" $e^{-\beta E_n}$ don't sum to one — they aren't probabilities at all. Expectation values would be off by a factor of $Z(\beta)$:

$$\text{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{A}] = Z(\beta)\cdot\langle\hat{A}\rangle_\beta$$

**In the purification approach**, the normalization is handled automatically. After imaginary time evolution $|\psi_\beta\rangle = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle$:

$$\langle\hat{O}\rangle_\beta = \frac{\langle\psi_\beta|\hat{O}|\psi_\beta\rangle}{\langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle}$$

The denominator $\langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle = Z(\beta)/Z(0)$ automatically provides the normalization.

## 16. Why a ladder structure? Why maximally entangled rungs?

### The ladder

The ladder preserves exactly the right structure:

| Property | Why the ladder preserves it |
|---|---|
| **Locality of $\hat{H}$** | $\hat{H}$ acts on $(1,3),(3,5),\ldots$ — next-nearest-neighbour, manageable with 4-site Trotter blocks |
| **MPS efficiency** | Entanglement between rungs is local; area law still holds |
| **Site correspondence** | Each physical site $i$ has exactly one auxiliary partner |
| **No inter-leg Hamiltonian** | $\hat{H}$ never acts on $Q$ — the auxiliary leg is inert |

### Why maximally entangled rungs?

The local density matrix at $\beta = 0$ is $\hat{\rho}_0^{(i)} = \frac{1}{d}\hat{I}_d$ — the maximally mixed state with von Neumann entropy $S = \log d$ (maximum possible). There is a precise theorem:

> A reduced state $\hat{\rho}^P$ has **equal eigenvalues** $\iff$ its purification $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_P\otimes\mathcal{H}_Q$ is **maximally entangled**.

Proof: Schmidt decomposition gives $|\psi^i_0\rangle = \sum_a \lambda_a |a\rangle_P|a\rangle_Q$. For $\hat{\rho}_0^{(i)} = \frac{1}{d}\hat{I}_d$ we need all $\lambda_a = 1/\sqrt{d}$, giving:

$$|\psi^i_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{\sigma=1}^d |\sigma\rangle_P|\sigma\rangle_Q$$

**Intuition:** Maximum classical ignorance (maximally mixed state on $P$) is encoded as maximum quantum entanglement between $P$ and $Q$ in the purification.

**Key practical consequence:** Despite each rung being maximally entangled, the global state has bond dimension $D=1$ — it is a product of rungs with no inter-rung entanglement at $\beta=0$. The entire finite-temperature computation is a controlled growth of this entanglement from $D=1$.

## 17. How does Eq. (236) hint at imaginary time evolution?

**Q:** Why does the author say we can see the need for imaginary time evolution based on how Eq. (236) looks?

**A:** The key is recognizing the mathematical structure of the operator that appears.

Substituting $\hat{I} = Z(0)\hat{\rho}_0 = Z(0)\text{Tr}_Q|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|$ into $\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z(\beta)^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\hat{I}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}$, and pulling the trace over $Q$ outside (since $\hat{H}$ does not act on $Q$):

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = \frac{Z(0)}{Z(\beta)}\,\text{Tr}_Q\!\left[\underbrace{e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle}_{?}\langle\psi_0|e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\right]$$

Compare with real time evolution:

| | Operator | Time argument |
|---|---|---|
| Real time evolution | $e^{-i\hat{H}t}$ | $t \in \mathbb{R}$ |
| Needed by Eq. (236) | $e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}$ | $\beta/2 \in \mathbb{R}^+$ |

These are identical in structure, related by $-it = -\beta/2 \implies t = -i\beta/2$. The absence of $i$ in the exponent is the direct hint — the expression is structurally a time evolution, but with imaginary time.

**Summary:**

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = \frac{Z(0)}{Z(\beta)} \xrightarrow{\text{split}} Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\hat{I}e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2} \xrightarrow{\text{purify}} \text{Tr}_Q\big[e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\big]$$

The expression demands you identify $e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle \equiv |\psi_\beta\rangle$ — which is imaginary time evolution with $\tau = \beta/2$.

## 18. Why is $\hat{I} = Z(0)\hat{\rho}_0$? Why is $Z(0) = d^L$?

### $\hat{I} = Z(0)\hat{\rho}_0$

This is just a rearrangement of the definition of the thermal density operator at $\beta = 0$:

$$\hat{\rho}_\beta = \frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}}}{Z(\beta)} \implies e^{-\beta\hat{H}} = Z(\beta)\hat{\rho}_\beta \xrightarrow{\beta=0} \hat{I} = Z(0)\hat{\rho}_0$$

### How tracing out $Q$ enters

Two distinct steps:
1. By definition of purification: $\text{Tr}_Q|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0| = \hat{\rho}_0$
2. Combined with step 1: $\hat{I} = Z(0)\hat{\rho}_0 = Z(0)\text{Tr}_Q|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|$

The trace alone gives $\hat{\rho}_0$; the factor $Z(0)$ is needed to recover $\hat{I}$.

### Why $Z(0) = d^L$

$Z(0) = \text{Tr}[e^{-0\cdot\hat{H}}] = \text{Tr}[\hat{I}] = \dim(\mathcal{H}) = d^L$.

For a chain of $L$ sites each with local Hilbert space dimension $d$, the total number of basis states (microstates) is $d^L$ — each site independently takes one of $d$ values. At $T=\infty$, all $d^L$ microstates are equally probable with weight 1, so the partition function just counts them.

## 19. Partition function and phase space volume

**Q:** Is there any relationship between the partition function and the volume of phase space?

**A:** Yes — one of the deepest connections in statistical mechanics.

**Classical:** The partition function is explicitly an integral over phase space:
$$Z_\text{classical}(\beta) = \frac{1}{h^{3N}N!}\int d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p\; e^{-\beta H(q,p)}$$

At $T\to\infty$ ($\beta\to 0$): $Z(0) = \text{total phase space volume}/h^{3N}N!$.

**Quantum:** The partition function is a Laplace transform of the density of states $\Omega(E)$:

$$Z(\beta) = \sum_n e^{-\beta E_n} = \int_0^\infty \Omega(E)\,e^{-\beta E}\,dE$$

| Object | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\Omega(E)$ | Number of microstates at energy $E$ — microcanonical |
| $Z(\beta)$ | Boltzmann-weighted sum over **all** states — canonical |
| $Z(\beta) = \int\Omega(E)e^{-\beta E}dE$ | Laplace transform connecting the two |

**Three limiting relationships:**

| Limit | $Z(\beta)$ | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $\beta\to 0$ ($T\to\infty$) | $\sum_n 1 = d^L$ | Unweighted count of **all** microstates |
| Finite $\beta$ | $\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}$ | Weighted count — high-energy states suppressed |
| $\beta\to\infty$ ($T\to 0$) | $g_0\,e^{-\beta E_0}$ | Counts only ground state ($g_0$ = degeneracy) |

**Boltzmann's formula:** $S_\text{max} = k_B\ln Z(0) = k_B\ln(d^L) = Lk_B\ln d$ — maximum entropy, $k_B$ times the log of the total number of configurations.

## 20. Why does $Z(\beta)/Z(0) = \langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle$?

**Q:** Why does quantum mechanics say $Z(\beta)/Z(0)$ is given by the square of the norm?

**A:** This follows from a direct computation. Express $|\psi_0\rangle$ in the energy eigenbasis of $\hat{H}$:

$$|\psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(0)}}\sum_n |n\rangle_P|\tilde{n}\rangle_Q$$

After imaginary time evolution:

$$|\psi_\beta\rangle = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}|\psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(0)}}\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n/2}|n\rangle_P|\tilde{n}\rangle_Q$$

Compute the norm squared:

$$\langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle = \frac{1}{Z(0)}\sum_{n,m} e^{-\beta E_n/2}e^{-\beta E_m/2} \underbrace{\langle m|n\rangle_P}_{\delta_{mn}} \underbrace{\langle\tilde{m}|\tilde{n}\rangle_Q}_{\delta_{mn}} = \frac{1}{Z(0)}\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n} = \frac{Z(\beta)}{Z(0)}$$

Schollwöck says "in complete agreement with standard quantum mechanics" because $|\psi_\beta\rangle$ is simply an **unnormalized** quantum state. For any unnormalized state $|\tilde{\psi}\rangle$, QM gives:

$$\langle\hat{O}\rangle = \frac{\langle\tilde{\psi}|\hat{O}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle}{\langle\tilde{\psi}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle}$$

The norm is not 1 because $e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}$ is not unitary — for non-unitary $\hat{A}$:

$$\langle\hat{A}\psi|\hat{A}\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{A}^\dagger\hat{A}|\psi\rangle \xrightarrow{\hat{A} = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}} \langle\psi_0|e^{-\beta\hat{H}}|\psi_0\rangle = \frac{Z(\beta)}{Z(0)}$$

## 21. How does accumulating normalization factors give $Z(\beta)$?

**Q:** Why does accumulating the normalization factors as temperature changes help us calculate $Z(\beta)$?

**A:** Imaginary time evolution proceeds in $N$ small steps of size $\Delta\beta = \beta/N$. At each step, the state is renormalized and the normalization factor is recorded:

| Step | Unnormalized state | Norm factor | Normalized state |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $\|\psi_0\rangle$, norm $= 1$ | $\alpha_0 = 1$ | $\|\tilde{\phi}_0\rangle = \|\psi_0\rangle$ |
| $k$ | $e^{-\Delta\beta\hat{H}/2}\|\tilde{\phi}_{k-1}\rangle$ | $\alpha_k = \sqrt{\langle\phi_k\|\phi_k\rangle}$ | $\|\tilde{\phi}_k\rangle = \|\phi_k\rangle/\alpha_k$ |

The true unnormalized state at the end is $|\psi_\beta\rangle = \left(\prod_{k=1}^N \alpha_k\right)|\tilde{\phi}_N\rangle$, so:

$$\langle\psi_\beta|\psi_\beta\rangle = \prod_{k=1}^N \alpha_k^2 = \frac{Z(\beta)}{Z(0)} \implies \boxed{Z(\beta) = d^L\cdot\prod_{k=1}^N \alpha_k^2}$$

Each factor $\alpha_k^2$ captures the ratio of partition functions between adjacent temperatures:

$$\alpha_k^2 \approx \frac{Z(\beta_k)}{Z(\beta_{k-1})}$$

The product telescopes to give $Z(\beta)/Z(0)$. You never need to diagonalize $\hat{H}$ or compute $\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}$ explicitly — the partition function emerges as a byproduct of the imaginary time evolution.

## 22. Intuitive meaning of $F(\beta)$; Helmholtz vs Gibbs free energy

### Intuitive meaning of $F$

$F$ is the **Helmholtz free energy**: $F(\beta) = U - TS = -\frac{1}{\beta}\ln Z(\beta)$.

It represents a competition:

| Term | Meaning | Favoured by |
|---|---|---|
| $U$ | Internal energy — system wants to minimise this | Low temperature |
| $-TS$ | Entropy gain — system wants to maximise disorder | High temperature |
| $F = U - TS$ | Net cost — nature minimises $F$ | Always |

At fixed $T$, $V$, $N$: the equilibrium state is the one that **minimises** $F$.

### Helmholtz vs Gibbs

| Quantity | Fixed variables | Expression | Includes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Helmholtz $F$ | $T, V, N$ | $F = U - TS$ | No $PV$ work |
| Gibbs $G$ | $T, P, N$ | $G = U - TS + PV = F + PV$ | $PV$ work |
| Grand potential $\Omega$ | $T, V, \mu$ | $\Omega = F - \mu N$ | Particle exchange |

### Why $F$ and not $G$ for the spin chain

For a quantum spin chain there is **no pressure and no volume** — spins sit on fixed lattice sites. Therefore:

$$G = F + PV = F + 0 = F$$

The canonical partition function $Z(\beta) = \text{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}]$ directly gives $F = -\beta^{-1}\ln Z(\beta)$. If the chain were coupled to a particle reservoir, replace $\hat{H} \to \hat{H} - \mu\hat{N}$ in the imaginary time evolution — the purification machinery is otherwise unchanged.

## 23. Why is the singlet advantageous? Why not doublet or triplet?

**Q:** Why is the local singlet state advantageous as the local purification? Why not doublet or triplet?

**A:** The two-spin Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_P^{(i)}\otimes\mathcal{H}_Q^{(i)}$ decomposes as $\frac{1}{2}\otimes\frac{1}{2} = 0 \oplus 1$. Check each state against the purification constraint $\text{Tr}_Q|\cdot\rangle\langle\cdot| = \frac{1}{2}\hat{I}$:

| State | $S$ | $S^z$ | $\text{Tr}_Q\|\cdot\rangle\langle\cdot\|$ | Valid purification? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\|\uparrow_P\uparrow_Q\rangle$ | $1$ | $+1$ | $\|\uparrow\rangle_P\langle\uparrow\|$ — pure state | **No** |
| $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\|\uparrow_P\downarrow_Q\rangle + \|\downarrow_P\uparrow_Q\rangle)$ | $1$ | $0$ | $\frac{1}{2}\hat{I}$ | Yes |
| $\|\downarrow_P\downarrow_Q\rangle$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $\|\downarrow\rangle_P\langle\downarrow\|$ — pure state | **No** |
| $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\|\uparrow_P\downarrow_Q\rangle - \|\downarrow_P\uparrow_Q\rangle)$ | $0$ | $0$ | $\frac{1}{2}\hat{I}$ | **Yes — singlet** |

The $|S^z=\pm 1\rangle$ triplet states fail immediately: they are product states, so tracing out $Q$ gives a pure state on $P$, not the maximally mixed state.

**Between singlet and $|S=1, S^z=0\rangle$ triplet:** Both are valid purifications. The singlet is preferred because Schollwöck states it "would allow to conserve total $S=0$ and $S^z=0$ at the same time." For an SU(2)-symmetric Hamiltonian:

| Starting state | $S^z_\text{total}$ conserved | $S_\text{total}$ conserved | Sector size |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\|S=1, S^z=0\rangle$ on each rung | $S^z=0$ ✓ | $S=1$, not $S=0$ ✗ | Moderate reduction |
| Singlet $\|S=0, S^z=0\rangle$ | $S^z=0$ ✓ | $S=0$ ✓ | **Maximum reduction** |

The singlet is also the **unique** $S=0$ state — the only one-dimensional irreducible representation of SU(2) for two spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particles.

The transition from the canonical purification to the singlet is a **local unitary on $Q$ alone**:

$$(\hat{I}_P\otimes i\sigma^y_Q)|\psi^i_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\uparrow\rangle_P|\downarrow\rangle_Q - |\downarrow\rangle_P|\uparrow\rangle_Q\right)$$

This preserves all structure (still a valid purification, still MPS $D=1$) while enabling quantum number conservation.

## 24. Why does the author say we need to find the MPO for pure state time evolution?

**Q:** Why does the author say we need to find the MPO to apply pure state time evolution at the end of §7.2.1? I thought we were doing mixed state time evolution.

**A:** This is the central payoff of purification. After §7.2.1, the mixed state problem has been **completely converted** into a pure state problem:

| Step | Object | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Start | $\hat{\rho}_\beta = Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$ | Mixed state on chain $P$ |
| Purify $\hat{\rho}_0$ | $\|\psi_0\rangle = \bigotimes_i\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_\sigma\|\sigma\rangle_P\|\sigma\rangle_Q$ | **Pure state** on ladder $P\otimes Q$ |
| Evolve | $\|\psi_\beta\rangle = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\|\psi_0\rangle$ | **Pure state** on ladder $P\otimes Q$ |
| Expectation values | $\langle\hat{O}\rangle_\beta = \langle\psi_\beta\|\hat{O}\|\psi_\beta\rangle/\langle\psi_\beta\|\psi_\beta\rangle$ | Pure state formula |

The §7.1 pure state time evolution algorithm applies directly:

| | §7.1 (real time, pure state) | §7.2 (imaginary time, purified) |
|---|---|---|
| State | $\|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}t}\|\psi_0\rangle$ | $\|\psi_\beta\rangle = e^{-\beta\hat{H}/2}\|\psi_0\rangle$ |
| State lives on | Single chain $P$ | Ladder $P\otimes Q$ |
| Substitution | $t \in \mathbb{R}$ | $t \to -i\beta/2$ |
| Algorithm | Trotter + MPO | **Same Trotter + MPO** |

The only new ingredient needed is the MPO appropriate for the ladder geometry — which §7.2.2 then constructs. The algorithm itself is unchanged.

> **One-line summary:** Purification converts mixed state evolution into pure state evolution on a bigger space. Once that mapping is done, you are simply evolving a pure MPS — the §7.1 algorithm applies unchanged.
